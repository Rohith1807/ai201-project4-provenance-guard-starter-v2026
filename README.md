## What This Does

<!-- The path a submission takes, from arriving to the label a reader sees.
     Six or seven steps — this is the trace you wrote in Milestone 1. -->

1. A caller sends a `POST /submit` request with `{"text": "...", "creator_id": "..."}` — the text enters the service as raw JSON.
2. The route handler in `app.py` parses the JSON, validates that `text` and `creator_id` are present and non-empty, and generates a unique `content_id`.
3. Signal one runs (`detector.model_signal`) — the GPT-2 model reads the text and returns a perplexity score, squashed to 0–1. Higher means more AI-like.
4. Signal two runs (`stylometry.style_signal`) — the text is analyzed as pure shape: sentence length variation, vocabulary variety, and punctuation density. Returns 0–1. Higher means more AI-like.
5. The two scores are combined (`scoring.combine_signals`) — a weighted rule produces one final confidence score between 0 and 1.
6. The score maps to a label (`scoring.score_to_label`) — the number becomes one of three human-readable verdicts: high-confidence human, unsure, or high-confidence AI.
7. The decision is written to the audit log (`audit.log_decision`) and the response is returned with `content_id`, `guess`, `confidence`, `label`, `model_score`, and `style_score`.

## Signals and Scoring

<!-- For each signal: what property it measures, why that might differ between
     human and AI writing, and WHAT IT CAN'T SEE.

     The blind spot is not optional. If you can't name it you don't understand
     the signal yet — and unit 8's attack set is built out of blind spots. -->

### Signal one — the model signal

**What it measures:** How predictable the text is to GPT-2, expressed as perplexity. Low perplexity means the model "could have guessed this"; high perplexity means the text surprised it. This is squashed to a 0–1 score where higher means more likely AI.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** AI text is generated by repeatedly picking the most probable next word, so a language model finds its own kind easy to predict. Human writing wanders — odd word choices, abrupt turns, the specific over the typical — producing higher perplexity.

**What it can't see:** Authorship. It measures predictability, not who wrote the text. Anyone writing in plain, simple English — a second-language writer sticking to vocabulary they're sure of, a younger writer, anyone writing for clarity — produces text just as predictable as AI output. The signal accuses the writers with the least power to argue back. That is structural, not a bug that can be fixed.

### Signal two — the style signal

**What it measures:** The shape of the text as text — how much sentence lengths vary (`sentence_length_spread`), how much vocabulary repeats (`type_token_ratio`), and how much punctuation appears (`punctuation_density`). It reads structure without understanding a word.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** Generated prose tends toward even, mid-length sentences and a narrower vocabulary band, because "most likely next word" averages out. Human writing is lumpier — a three-word sentence next to a forty-word one, a dash of unusual punctuation, a sudden shift in rhythm.

**What it can't see:** Anything about what the text means. It has no idea what the text says. Anything that changes the shape without changing the substance defeats it completely — adding typos raises word variety, breaking one sentence into two raises spread, pasting in a short quotation disrupts the rhythm. This is a trivially cheap manipulation that costs an attacker nothing.

### The combining rule

<!-- Write the rule in words, then give the numbers. What happens when the two
     signals disagree? -->

**The rule:** Weighted average of the two signals, with the model signal weighted higher because it is harder to game — defeating it requires writing in a way a language model finds surprising, which is a real constraint. The style signal is defeated by cheap surface edits (typos, sentence breaks), so it earns less weight. When the two signals strongly disagree (gap > 0.35), the combined score is nudged 20% of that gap toward whichever signal reads more human, implementing the false-positive preference from criteria.md: wrong accusations cost more than missed detections.

**The numbers:** model signal weight = 0.65, style signal weight = 0.35. Strong-disagreement threshold = 0.35.

**Where it lives:** `scoring.py::combine_signals`

**A case where my two signals split, and what my rule does with it:** "Clearly human" text (personal, conversational writing) scored model=0.326 (human) but style=0.635 (AI-leaning), a gap of 0.309. The style signal misread it because the text is short (~53 words), so type-token ratio is high across the board and sentence spread is low. The weighted average lands at 0.434 (human), which is correct — the model signal's clear human reading pulls the combined score below the 0.5 threshold despite the style signal disagreeing.

| unsure | 0.47 – 0.67 | Our signals don't agree on this one. It has features we associate with human writing and features we associate with AI-generated text. We're not confident either way. If you wrote this yourself, no action is needed — but you can appeal if this result affects you. |
| high-confidence AI | 0.67 – 1.00 | This submission closely matches the patterns we see in AI-generated text — predictable word choices and an even, regular style. We think it was written by an AI. If you wrote this yourself, you can appeal using the content ID in this response. |

**Why I worded the "unsure" one this way:** The unsure label has to do two things that pull against each other: it has to be honest about uncertainty, and it must not read as an accusation. "Our signals don't agree on this one" puts the uncertainty on the system, not the writer. The second sentence tells them what to expect without implying they did something wrong. The appeal line at the end gives them an action only if the result actually affects them — it doesn't assume it does.

## How I Used AI

**Moment 1**

- *What I asked for:* Help implementing the three stylometry measures in `stylometry.py` — specifically how to combine `sentence_length_spread`, `type_token_ratio`, and `punctuation_density` into a single 0–1 score pointing in the right direction.
- *What came back:* A working implementation using coefficient of variation for spread, raw TTR, and a logistic squash for punctuation density. It set weights at 0.45/0.40/0.15 and flipped all three so higher = more AI-like.
- *What I changed:* The logistic parameters. The initial midpoints for the punctuation squash (0.005) were guesses — after running the four calibration inputs, I could see punctuation density across all of them was near zero, which meant punctuation was contributing almost nothing. I confirmed this by printing each sub-score separately. The weight structure stayed, but understanding why punctuation was flat helped me decide to leave it at 0.15 rather than raise it.

**Moment 2**

- *What I asked for:* Help setting the combining rule in `scoring.py::combine_signals` — specifically what to do when the two signals strongly disagree, and which signal should win.
- *What came back:* A weighted average (0.65 model, 0.35 style) with a disagreement bias: when the gap exceeds 0.35, pull the combined score 20% of the gap toward whichever signal reads more human.
- *What I changed:* The threshold at which the bias kicks in. The first version used 0.4 as the gap threshold, which was too high — none of the calibration inputs triggered it. I lowered it to 0.35 so it would activate on the "clearly human" text (model=0.326, style=0.635, gap=0.309), which is exactly the false-positive scenario the rule was meant to protect against. I also checked that it didn't change the AI text results, which it didn't.

## Rate Limiting

**My limits:** 5 per minute, 100 per day

**Why those numbers and not others:** A real writer submitting a draft, revising, and resubmitting might plausibly send 3–5 times in a single sitting. Five per minute comfortably covers that. A script probing thresholds hits the wall after five rapid-fire submissions — the very pattern threshold-mapping requires. 100 per day gives a prolific human writer room to work all day while making a full automated sweep of the threshold space (which needs hundreds of variations) expensive enough to slow down without stopping a determined attacker entirely.

**What a caller counts as:** Per `creator_id` — not per IP address. This matters: one IP can spawn thousands of `creator_id` values and look like a crowd, but one household sharing an IP all look like one caller. The service reads `creator_id` from the JSON body; if it's missing or not a string, it falls back to the caller's IP. Code: `app.py::rate_limit_key`.

## The Improvement
